Coming Back.

A hundred years ago there were more than fifty salmon streams running through Vancouver. Most were buried. A handful are now coming back.

Under the streets of East Vancouver, under Kingsway, under the parking lot behind the Canadian Tire on Grandview Highway, there is a river system. It ran salmon. It carried the drainage of the whole city into False Creek and Burrard Inlet. Most of it is now a pipe.

Between the 1880s and the 1970s, Vancouver buried roughly 120 kilometres of stream. The engineering was routine. The reasoning was land use. The result is a modern city built directly on top of a working salmon watershed.

This is the story of what was buried, why it was buried, and what has begun — creek by creek — to come back.

The term

What "daylighting" actually means

Daylighting is the deliberate removal of a stream from the pipe, culvert, or concrete channel it was buried in, and its return to an open, vegetated channel at the surface — re-exposing a watercourse to daylight after decades or a century underground.

The term entered North American planning vocabulary through American Rivers' Daylighting Streams: Breathing Life Into Urban Streams and Communities, which remains the standard reference text for practitioners.

01

What gets removed

A buried stream typically sits inside one of three things: a combined sewer (carrying stormwater and sanitary waste in the same pipe), a storm-only culvert, or a hard-lined concrete flume. Daylighting means taking that structure out — or bypassing it — and cutting a new channel where the water can run open.

It is not the same as stream restoration (improving an already-open stream) or naturalization (softening an existing channel). Daylighting is specifically the un-burying step.

02

What gets built

An open channel alone is not habitat. Working projects pair the excavation with riparian planting (native willow, hardhack, sedge along the banks), gravel substrate sized for spawning, large woody debris for pool-and-riffle structure, and often biofiltration cells upstream to treat road runoff before it reaches the new channel.

Where fish passage is the goal, culvert crossings downstream must also be corrected — a daylighted reach with a perched culvert below it is inaccessible to salmon.

03

Why it matters ecologically

A piped stream performs almost none of the functions of a stream. It moves water fast, in the dark, over a smooth invert, with no light, no plants, no gravel, and no insects. Returning it to the surface restores the physical conditions that aquatic ecosystems require:

  • Shading and thermal buffering — riparian canopy over an open channel moderates water temperature, which is the limiting factor for salmonid survival in urban streams.
  • Floodplain reconnection — an open channel with room to spill absorbs peak flows instead of transmitting them downstream at full velocity.
  • Habitat complexity — gravel, wood, pools and riffles support invertebrate communities, which in turn support fish and birds.
  • Infiltration and recharge — water in contact with soil returns to groundwater rather than being flushed to the outfall.

The strongest peer-reviewed evidence for the temperature pathway is Jorgensen et al. (2022), PLOS ONE, which found that combined riparian shading and floodplain reconnection produced the largest modelled improvement in stream thermal conditions. Note: that study is Washington State, not British Columbia — it is cited here as a regional analogue, not as BC-specific evidence.

04

Why cities do it

Ecology is rarely the whole business case. Municipalities daylight streams for reasons that stack:

  • Aging infrastructure. A century-old culvert reaching end-of-life must be replaced regardless. Daylighting competes against the cost of a new pipe, not against zero.
  • Sewer capacity. Removing clean stormwater from a combined system reduces overflow events during heavy rain.
  • Flood management. Surface conveyance with floodplain storage handles peak events that pipes size-limited to a design storm cannot.
  • Regulatory obligation. Fish-bearing waters carry federal Fisheries Act obligations; restoration can be required or credited.
  • Public space. A daylit creek corridor is a linear park. That value is real, and it is what usually wins the council vote.

What daylighting does not do

It does not offset climate warming. Restoration mitigates local thermal and hydrological stress within a reach; it does not counteract regional air-temperature increase, and studies that model restoration benefits generally show them narrowing under high-emissions scenarios. It is also not linear — Still Creek's own salmon record includes a multi-year gap after 2016, and recovery in one reach can be undone by a barrier downstream.

Claims circulating in popular coverage that this piece does not repeat, because they could not be verified against a primary source: a specific "5.9°C cooling" figure attributed to daylighted streams; a "20–80% macroinvertebrate increase"; and the widely-repeated Vancouver folk claim that only two visible streams remain in the city. CHAPTER 01 · DISCOVERY

Before Vancouver was built, this is what ran through it.

In 1978, aquarium biologist Sharon Proctor sat down with old maps, archival photographs, and interviews with elderly Vancouverites who remembered fishing in Kitsilano and Mount Pleasant as boys. Out of that came a hand-drawn map of Vancouver's original streams: roughly 120 kilometres of surface water, more than fifty of them known salmon streams, threading between what would become the West End and the Fraser.

UBC digitized the map in 2011. The result is the layer you are looking at — 178 stream segments, 13 of them still known by name: China Creek, Brewery Creek, Vivian Creek, MacDonald Creek, Musqueam Creek, and the survivor of the whole system, Still Creek.

50+ Salmon streams recorded in Vancouver in the 1880s, before industrial-scale burial began.

Proctor's reconstruction cross-referenced archival survey maps, fire-insurance atlases, and historical photographs against the memories of long-time residents who had known the creeks as open water in childhood. It remains the most complete picture of Vancouver's pre-development hydrology that exists — and it is a reconstruction, not a survey, so segment positions carry the uncertainty of a map partly drawn from memory and century-old paper.

UBC's 2011 digitization georeferenced the hand-drawn original to modern coordinates. That is what makes it a layer you can lay buildings and sewers on top of — as the next chapters do. Click any stream to see its name and where it ran.

CHAPTER 02 · DISCOVERY → DAMAGE

One by one, we buried them.

Vancouver was engineered on top of its streams, not around them. As the grid pushed out from Gastown between the 1880s and the 1930s, the streams became a problem: they cut across the grid on a diagonal it could not accommodate, they flooded basements in wet winters, they carried the sewage of whoever lived upstream, and every crossing needed a bridge or a culvert that someone had to pay for.

The answer, again and again, was the combined sewer — a single pipe carrying stormwater and sanitary waste together, laid directly in the old streambed. It was the standard of the era, and it was chosen for exactly the reason it is now a liability: it was the cheapest way to make a stream vanish while still moving the water somewhere.

By the 1970s most of the network had been culverted, filled, or paved. The streams didn't disappear. They kept flowing — in the dark, at speed, into the same receiving waters as before.

How a creek becomes a pipe
  1. Divert. Flow is intercepted upstream and routed into a new trunk line.
  2. Culvert. The channel itself is encased — brick, then concrete, then reinforced pipe as materials changed decade to decade.
  3. Backfill. The ravine or gully above the culvert is filled, often with construction spoil and municipal waste.
  4. Build. Once the surface is level and drained, the lots are subdivided and the grid closes over the top.

Each step is reasonable on its own. Together they leave the stream still flowing — now legally and physically part of the sewer system.

The named creeks each went a different way. China Creek drained a substantial ravine through what is now Mount Pleasant and Grandview; it was progressively culverted and its ravine filled — the corridor now runs largely under Kingsway and the Grandview Cut. Brewery Creek, which once powered a brewery near Main and 7th, was piped into the sewer network as Mount Pleasant densified. Vivian, MacDonald, and the Kitsilano drainages went under with the West Side subdivision boom.

A combined sewer runs to treatment on a dry day. But its capacity is fixed, and a heavy Vancouver rain can exceed it — at which point the system is designed to spill the excess, a mix of stormwater and sewage, straight to the creeks, the Fraser, or Burrard Inlet. These combined sewer overflows are the single largest reason the city is now spending billions to separate the two flows. Daylighting a stream pulls clean stormwater out of that pipe entirely — which is why it lives in the city's sewer strategy, not only its parks plan.

CHAPTER 03 · DAMAGE

When we buried them, we buried what they gave us.

A salmon stream is not only a salmon stream. Burying one removes a set of physical functions at once — and each loss compounds the others.

What a pipe cannot do
  • Slow water down. A smooth invert moves stormwater fast, with no floodplain to spill into — the mechanism behind urban flash flooding.
  • Keep water cool. No canopy, no shade. Runoff off hot asphalt enters warm and stays warm.
  • Hold life. No light, no gravel, no insects. A culvert is biologically close to inert.
  • Pass fish. Perched outfalls and dark barrels block migration even where water quality would allow it.

The consequences are still visible. Vancouver's designated floodplain — the coral footprint on the map — tracks the low ground the old streams once drained. The 2021 atmospheric river and heat dome both landed on a city that had traded a natural stormwater-and-cooling system for a pipe network built for neither.

The salmon loss is the one that became invisible. A run doesn't collapse loudly when the water it needs is sealed under a road — it just stops being observed, then stops being remembered.

Flood, heat, no fish. The three losses that map most directly to the buried-streams footprint.

CHAPTER 04 · THE RETURN

Eighty years absent. Then the salmon came back.

Still Creek runs 17 kilometres across East Vancouver and Burnaby — the one substantial stream Vancouver never finished burying. Most is culverted, but daylighted sections remain along Cornett Road and behind the industrial parcels on Grandview Highway.

In November 2012, chum salmon spawned there for the first time in roughly eighty years. It happened behind a Canadian Tire — because, starting quietly with a 1988 Council policy and then in earnest after 2002, three governments had slowly rebuilt the creek: pulling concrete flumes, planting willow and hardhack, mandating 15-to-30-metre streamside corridors at every major redevelopment. In fall 2024, chum returned again — the strongest run in years.

2012 · 2024 The return years that broke the eighty-year absence. Real — but fragile: some years, no fish reached the Vancouver reach.
Play the timeline. It walks the policy chain that produced this — ending with the Rupert & Renfrew Station Area Plan (approved July 8, 2025), which commits Vancouver to more Still Creek daylighting.

Restoration was not a single project but two decades of accumulating work by three governments and a community organization. Concrete flumes were pulled and replaced with naturalized channel. Native willow and hardhack were planted to shade and stabilize the banks. Biofiltration ponds were built to treat road runoff before it reached the creek.

The quiet lever was land-use policy. Redevelopment along the corridor — including large sites like Bentall and Costco — was required to hold a 15-to-30-metre streamside corridor, so the creek gained room every time a parcel turned over. The Still Moon Arts Society, active since 2003, ran the streamkeeping and salmon-spotting that kept it going between capital projects.

It remains fragile. In 2017–18 no salmon reached the Vancouver reach at all — fish were stopped at the Boundary Road culvert, a reminder that a single downstream barrier can undo an entire restored corridor.

Chum salmon returning to Still Creek

What we know: chum returned in 2012 after ~80 years, spawned consecutively in the Vancouver reach through 2016, disappeared in 2017–18 (blocked at the Boundary Rd culvert), returned intermittently, then spawned abundantly in fall 2024. What we don't know: DFO NuSEDS doesn't publish year-by-year counts for the Still Creek reach; the shape above tracks the qualitative record from Still Moon Arts + city + press coverage, not audited counts.

CHAPTER 05 · IT'S NOT JUST ONE CREEK

Thirteen projects, three governments, one method.

In July 2024, a 200-metre daylighted section of what was historically called First Creek opened at Tatlow Park in Kitsilano — engineered by KWL, delivered under Vancouver's Rain City Strategy, and awarded a 2025 CSLA National Honour Award. In summer 2025, Canyon Creek opened at Spanish Banks: about 85 metres of new open water, 65 parking stalls converted to habitat, funded by $992,800 from the federal Nature Infrastructure Program plus $1,175,818 from the City.

Older precedents run in the same direction — Guichon Creek through the BCIT campus, Hoy and Scott Creeks in Coquitlam feeding a community-run hatchery, the Brunette River basin's 73 square kilometres across five cities where Chinook, Coho, and Sockeye are again reaching Stoney, Deer, Eagle and Beecher creeks.

CHAPTER 06 · RECOVERY AT SCALE

Victoria is unburying a stream over a century.

Bowker Creek runs 8 kilometres through Saanich, Victoria, and Oak Bay. Two-thirds of it is buried in pipes. In 2003 the three municipalities and the Capital Regional District signed onto a Watershed Management Plan. In 2011 they adopted the Bowker Creek Blueprint — a 100-year plan to progressively daylight the creek as buildings, roads, and pipes reach end-of-life above it.

In 2015, KWL engineered the first 120 metres of daylighting at Oak Bay High School. In 2020, ISL Engineering completed a further feasibility study for corridor-scale work. The Blueprint itself is due for an update in 2026 — the milestone that timed this piece.

100 yrs Length of Victoria's Bowker Creek Blueprint. The daylighting is scheduled to happen as everything above the creek turns over.
The 100-year timeline

  • 2011 — the three councils adopt the Bowker Creek Blueprint: a 100-year staged-daylighting plan keyed to infrastructure renewal — the creek is unburied as the buildings, roads and pipes above it reach end-of-life, not all at once.

CHAPTER 07 · WHAT SCALES THIS

Policy, money, and where the money comes from.

Three levers explain why Vancouver has more daylit stream today than a decade ago. First: the Rain City Strategy, requiring most new development to capture the first 48 millimetres of rainfall on site. Second: corridor-setback rules at station-area plans like Rupert & Renfrew — approved 8 July 2025 — which reserve 15-to-30 metres either side of Still Creek for restoration when adjacent parcels redevelop. Third: money.

British Columbia's Watershed Security Fund, run through the Real Estate Foundation of BC and the Healthy Watersheds Initiative, has distributed roughly $18 million across three intake rounds since 2024 — $5M, $7M, and $6M — with recent grants to Soowahlie First Nation for Sweltzer Creek and to Redd Fish Restoration Society on Vancouver Island.

Internationally, the cost range is wide. French daylighting has run €320 to €2,600 per metre. Taiwanese projects have been reported at €30,000 to €45,000 per metre. Large-scale US work in the mid-2000s ran roughly $1,180 in 2026 dollars per linear foot. What determines the number is not the ecology — it's what has to be moved out of the way.

CHAPTER 08 · COMING BACK

There are streams under this city. They can come back.

The evidence is now on the map. Still Creek behind the Canadian Tire. Tatlow at Kitsilano. Canyon Creek at Spanish Banks. Guichon at BCIT. Hoy and Scott through Coquitlam's Linear Park. Bowker over a century. Sweltzer under a First Nations restoration grant. What used to be a single-site experiment is becoming a method.

Vancouver's buried streams have never stopped flowing. Turning that flow back into stream — creek by creek, corridor by corridor — is now a documented, funded, replicable practice. It is slow. It is not universal. But it is happening, and it works.

Methodology

How this piece was reported, and what it deliberately leaves out

Every quantitative claim in this piece was checked against a primary or institutional source before publication. Claims that could not be traced to such a source were removed rather than softened — including several that circulate widely in popular coverage of stream daylighting.

Historical stream network
Sharon Proctor's 1978 mapping for the Vancouver Aquarium, compiled from archival maps, photographs, and interviews with long-time residents. Digitized by Lesack & Proctor (2011) and published through UBC Library. 178 segments, 13 carrying names.
Infrastructure layers
Sewer mains and trunk lines from City of Vancouver and Metro Vancouver open data portals under their respective open licences. Floodplain from City of Vancouver designated floodplain dataset.
Building footprints
OpenStreetMap, retrieved July 2026 for the City of Vancouver extent. Heights use tagged height where present, otherwise building:levels × 3.2 m, otherwise an 8 m default. Present-day footprints — not a historical construction record.
Project inventory
Compiled from municipal project pages, engineering-firm project records, regional district documentation, awards citations, and contemporaneous news coverage. Costs are quoted only where a public figure exists.

Claims excluded after verification

The following appear in circulating coverage of daylighting but could not be substantiated against a primary source, and are not asserted anywhere in this piece:

  • A specific “5.9 °C” cooling effect attributed to daylighted streams.
  • A “20–80% macroinvertebrate increase” following daylighting.
  • The claim that only two visible streams remain in urban Vancouver — a persistent local folk claim.
  • Specific programme figures for Zurich's daylighting programme.
  • Quantified economic-benefit figures for Arcadia Creek, Kalamazoo.
  • A $250 M endowment figure for the BC Watershed Security Fund. Distributed grant totals are used instead.

Known gaps

Three gaps are worth stating plainly, because they bound what this piece can claim. First, there is no published parcel-level record of Vancouver's stream-burial sequence between 1880 and 1970; the chronology here reflects the general documented pattern of culverting and grid expansion, not dated construction events. Second, DFO's NuSEDS database does not publish year-by-year escapement counts for the Still Creek reach, so the salmon-return chart in Chapter 4 represents the qualitative published record — city communications, Still Moon Arts reporting, and press coverage — not audited counts. Third, the strongest peer-reviewed evidence cited for the thermal-restoration pathway (Jorgensen et al., 2022) is from Washington State and is used as a regional analogue; BC-specific quantitative work on urban daylighting outcomes remains thin.
