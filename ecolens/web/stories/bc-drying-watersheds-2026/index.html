Below Normal.

Three years of shrinking snowpacks, decades of clearcut logging, and a water-pricing system stuck in the 20th century. How British Columbia's watersheds came apart in 2026 — and what's already coming back.

By Laurence Magenga For EcoLens Reviewed by —

Provincial mountain snowpack as a percentage of normal, June 15, 2026. By mid-June, the Snow Basin Index reached zero across Vancouver Island, the South Coast, and the Boundary region. Summer hadn't started.

British Columbia's water system depends on snow acting as delayed storage — accumulating through winter at elevation, then melting slowly through spring and summer to feed rivers when the rain stops. Roughly half of the province's annual precipitation is held this way.

In recent years, that storage has arrived weaker, melted earlier, and in some basins vanished entirely before summer even began. This is a province-wide pattern of low flows, salmon stress, municipal water emergencies, and an economic architecture that has not yet caught up.

What follows is a six-part investigation. It moves from the provincial baseline down to a single Sunshine Coast watershed, out to the salmon streams being triaged with $200 cameras, north to a city building a $100 million emergency pipeline, and ends at the $2.25-per-million-litre rate that is quietly at the root of it all.

SECTION 01 · THE MACRO-COLLAPSE

The snowpack has already failed — across the whole province.

British Columbia's hydrology is fundamentally governed by the cryosphere. Mountain snowpack acts as a natural frozen reservoir, storing roughly half of the province's annual precipitation. Under stable conditions, it releases water slowly from May through August, keeping rivers cool for migrating salmon and pressure in municipal intakes.

In 2026, that baseline shattered. Consecutive years of extreme summer heat, dry autumns, and low winter snow accumulations culminated in a catastrophic spring melt.

0% Snow Basin Index across Vancouver Island, the South Coast, and the Boundary region by June 15, 2026. The snow didn't just melt early — in many critical high-elevation zones, it vanished before summer officially began.

SECTION 02 · INDUSTRIAL CLEARCUTS

The Wilson Creek case study.

Climate change dictates what falls from the sky. Industrial forest management dictates how the land handles it. On the Sunshine Coast, in the ts'ukw'um (Wilson Creek) watershed, decades of intensive clearcut logging have fundamentally altered the landscape's ability to act as a sponge.

When a mature canopy is removed, winter snow melts up to 50% faster because it is directly exposed to solar radiation. Compact, root-depleted soil loses its ability to retain groundwater. What was slow release becomes flash flood in winter and dry creek bed in summer.

55% Equivalent Clearcut Area in Wilson Creek — nearly double the 30% threshold the province recognizes as high hydrological risk.

UBC hydrologist Dr. Younes Alila's research shows a regenerating second-growth forest takes 60 to 80 years to recover just 90% of its original hydrological function — a multi-generational "hydrological debt" downstream communities pay in floods and droughts.

[ Canopy removal ] → [ Sun exposes snowpack ] → [ Fast spring runoff ] → [ Summer desiccation ] ↓ Soil compaction + loss of root matrix → Flash floods + zero groundwater recharge

SECTION 03 · SALMON TRIAGE

The $200 protocol replacing $50,000 hydrometric stations.

As rivers dwindle to a trickle, water temperatures frequently break the critical 20°C threshold — causing immediate physiological distress, oxygen depletion, and pre-spawning mortality in Pacific salmon. Managing that crisis is severely crippled by a data deficit: provincial and federal hydrometric stations cost tens of thousands of dollars each, leaving thousands of sensitive salmon streams completely unmonitored.

$200 Per-site cost of the trail-camera + staff-gauge setup used in the Pacific Salmon Foundation's Rapid Drought Assessment Protocol. Deployed by local streamkeepers and First Nations partners.

SECTION 04 · MUNICIPAL VS INDUSTRIAL

Dawson Creek's emergency, and where the water is actually going.

In Northeast BC, the city of Dawson Creek (population ~15,000) relies entirely on the highly volatile Kiskatinaw River for its drinking water. In early 2024, the river's flows dropped so low that the city's intake pumps sat frozen and dry for nearly two months.

$100M Fast-tracked 55-kilometre emergency pipeline from the Peace, Pine, or Murray rivers. EAO approval compressed from 5 years to 2.

The paradox is what sits in the same basin. Dawson Creek's water source is at the heart of the Montney Formation — one of the most water-intensive industrial extraction zones in North America. Hydraulic fracturing (fracking) operations pull millions of litres of fresh groundwater and surface water permanently out of the cycle.

SECTION 05 · THE NORTHERN PARADOX

Two water stories, one province.

British Columbia operates under two entirely conflicting water realities at the exact same time. More than 70% of the province's electricity is generated by massive hydroelectric installations on the Peace and Columbia River networks. From 2022 to 2025, northern drought turned BC Hydro from a historical exporter into a massive net importer — 13,600 GWh imported in 2023/24 alone, at a cost of $1.4 billion.

Full Williston Reservoir status by late June 2026 — while Vancouver Island and the South Coast sat at 0% snowpack. Macro-reservoir health does not translate to ecological water security.

This creates a dangerous illusion of security. Because the multi-billion-dollar energy reservoirs look full, the true state of ecological water depletion across the rest of the province stays hidden from public view.

SECTION 06 · ECONOMICS OF SCARCITY

$2.25 vs $155 per million litres.

The foundational root of BC's watershed insecurity is an outdated economic framework that severely undervalues water as a finite resource. Under the current rate structure, the province charges large industrial users a maximum of $2.25 per million litres of fresh water withdrawn from natural sources — the lowest industrial water rate in Canada.

[ BC · $2.25 / ML ] → $400M total revenue · 0% legally to watersheds [ Quebec · $155 / ML ] → funds Blue Fund · security investments [ Nova Scotia · $179 ] → dedicated water conservation revenue

A fracking operation can inject millions of litres of pristine water for less than $60 in rental fees. BC generates roughly $400 million annually in water rentals — 97% of which comes from BC Hydro operations — and 0% is legally earmarked for watershed protection.

$3-5M Annual interest generated by the Watershed Security Fund — compared with the $100 million per year experts estimate is needed to climate-proof BC's communities and salmon ecosystems.
