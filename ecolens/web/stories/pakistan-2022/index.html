One-Third Under Water

In summer 2022, monsoon rains and accelerated glacial melt put a third of Pakistan beneath floodwater. 33 million people were affected. 16 million were children. This map tracks the hazard, then follows the Pakistani NGOs, local networks, fishers, volunteers, and advocates who responded.

Section 1 of 5

Pakistan, briefly

Before the flood, the place. The South Asian country of 251 million, fifth-largest population on Earth, fed by the Indus and bordered by the Himalayas, the Hindu Kush, the Arabian Sea, and four neighbours.

Story thread Follow the water: from glaciers in Hunza, down the Indus, into the Dadu-Manchar floodplain, then into the relief camps and recovery sites.

Where in the world

Pakistan sits at the crossroads of South Asia, Central Asia, and the Middle East — bordering India, Afghanistan, Iran, and China, with a coastline on the Arabian Sea. Total area: 881,913 km², divided into four provinces — Punjab, Sindh, Khyber-Pakhtunkhwa, and Balochistan — plus the federal capital territory of Islamabad and the autonomous regions of Gilgit-Baltistan and Azad Kashmir.

251 million people

Pakistan is the fifth most populous country on Earth (251.3 million as of 2024) — and one of the youngest, median age 20.8, with more than half under 25.

Population concentrates in three spines: the Indus plain in Punjab (Lahore, Faisalabad, Multan), the lower Indus in Sindh (Karachi, Hyderabad, Sukkur), and a smaller corridor through KP (Peshawar). Balochistan, the largest province by area, is the least densely populated.

What people do

Agriculture is the spine: 22.9% of GDP, 37.4% of the workforce. Pakistan is the world's 4th-largest cotton producer, the 7th-largest wheat producer, and a major rice + sugarcane exporter. Almost all of it is irrigated by the Indus basin, which provides ~80% of the country's water.

Industry concentrates in Karachi (port, finance, textiles), Faisalabad (textile manufacturing), Lahore (cultural + second-largest economy). 2024 GDP: $346 billion.

The geography that matters for what comes next

Three terrain facts will become important: the Indus drains from the world's third-largest concentration of glaciers in the Karakoram and Hindu Kush; the lower Indus flows through a flat, irrigated plain across Punjab and Sindh; and the Indus delta is dam-starved — so the river barely reaches the sea in normal years.

Section 2 of 5

The disaster

A pre-monsoon heatwave melted glaciers. The monsoon brought 190% of normal rainfall. Centuries-old water infrastructure failed. From mid-June to October 2022, one-third of Pakistan was under water.

Before the rain — the stage was already set

The 2022 disaster did not begin in mid-June. By the time the first heavy rains arrived, both the climate dial and the structural baseline were already loaded against Pakistan. Climate-system stress had been building for months; structural fragility for decades.

The map is auto-touring through the pre-flood events. Each pin is a discrete contributing factor — click any for the citation.

Structural / governance (pre-existing fragility)
ongoing Tarbela + Mangla dams silted to ~25–40% of original storage — buffer capacity against monsoon spikes already eroded.
2000–2022 KP + Gilgit-Baltistan lost ~27% of forest cover (Hansen GFC v1.11). National forest cover ~4.8% — among Asia's lowest. Less canopy = faster runoff.
2010–2022 2010 floods reconstruction never completed — many embankments and homes destroyed then were never rebuilt to higher resilience standards. Same villages hit again in 2022.
FY18–22 Climate Change Authority budget < $5M/year — Pakistan has no FEMA-equivalent disaster reconstruction fund.
Q1–Q2 2022 IMF Extended Fund Facility stalled — federal liquidity already strained, no spare capacity for pre-emptive monsoon spending.
9 April 2022 PM Imran Khan ousted by no-confidence vote — cabinet transition + IMF re-negotiations distracted federal government from monsoon preparedness during the critical pre-monsoon window.
Climate triggers (2022-specific)
2020–2022 Triple-dip La Niña — rare third consecutive year. Caused anomalous easterly winds across the subcontinent. Nanditha et al. (2023) link this directly to the 2022 monsoon intensity via a Rossby wave pattern.
~2018 ongoing Karakoram anomaly reversal — historically advancing glaciers now losing ~6 Gt/yr (NASA GRACE-FO, Wiese et al. 2024). The frozen reservoir is shrinking.
Mar – Jun 2022 Arabian Sea SST anomaly — surface temperatures ran 1.5–2 °C above the 1981–2010 average. Warmer sea = more evaporation = stronger southerly moisture flow into Sindh and Balochistan.
Mar – May 2022 Pakistan + NW India hottest March in 122 years. Jacobabad hit 51 °C in June. Pre-monsoon heat dried out topsoil (less infiltration) and accelerated upstream glacial melt.
7 May 2022 Shisper Glacier outburst (GLOF) in Hunza destroyed the historic Hassanabad Bridge — three months before the Sindh floods downstream. Same crisis, opposite ends of the country.
Mar – Aug 2022 16 documented GLOFs across Gilgit-Baltistan and KP, pre-saturating Indus tributaries before the monsoon arrived.
Mid-June 2022 Early monsoon onset — ~2 weeks earlier than the long-term average, overlapping with the tail of the pre-monsoon heatwave and the GLOF surge.

Then on June 14, the rain started.

Sources: PMD · UNDP GLOF-II · ICIMOD HIMAP · Hansen GFC v1.11 · NASA GRACE-FO (Wiese et al. 2024) · IMF Pakistan country report Apr 2022 · Nanditha et al. 2023, Earth's Future.

The heat came first — and the glaciers

Before the rain there was the heat. In Jacobabad, Sindh, the thermometer hit 51 °C in June 2022 — one of the highest temperatures ever recorded on Earth. The same heatwave was melting glaciers 1,500 km to the north, in the high Karakoram and Hindu Kush.

Pakistan holds the largest concentration of glaciers outside the polar regions — about 7,253. The 2022 heat pre-saturated the Indus tributary system before the monsoon arrived, and triggered at least 16 documented Glacial Lake Outburst Floods (GLOFs), including the catastrophic Shisper Glacier outburst on 7 May 2022, which destroyed the historic Hassanabad Bridge in Hunza.

Then 190% of normal rain

The 2022 monsoon delivered 190% of normal rainfall across Pakistan. Sindh alone received 466% of its 30-year August average. Balochistan received 500% more than its August normal.

The peer-reviewed climate-attribution study — Otto et al. (2023) in Environmental Research: Climate — concluded that the 5-day maximum rainfall over Sindh and Balochistan was ~75% more intense than it would have been without human-caused warming. Pakistan contributed less than 1% of the global emissions driving that warming.

The water spread — 3D flood simulation

Through July and August the water spread south down the Indus floodplain. By the end of August, an area larger than the United Kingdom was under water. UNOSAT's weekly satellite snapshots show the suffocating geography of the disaster.

Structural pressure — Sukkur Barrage + Manchar Lake

The lower Indus was never only a river channel. It was barrages, canals, drains, embankments, roads, and an extremely flat floodplain. In 2022, extreme local rainfall and upstream flows filled that system faster than it could drain, turning protective infrastructure into a chain of hard choices.

The Manchar Lake protective embankment was deliberately breached on 5 September to save Sehwan town — flooding ~150,000 people in Bhan Saeedabad. A textbook "sacrifice zone" decision still controversial in 2026.

Forest cover across the upper Indus catchment is ~4.8% of land area, among the lowest in Asia; KP and Gilgit-Baltistan lost ~27% of their forest cover from 2000–2020 per Hansen Global Forest Change. There was nothing to slow the water down.

Section 3 of 5

Who and what was under the water

33 million people affected. 16 million children. 8.2 million women. 650,000 pregnant. Plus the Indus dolphin habitat, Manchar Lake, the riverine forests, and the Indus delta. The flood was a population disaster and an ecological one.

The exposure map — population in the flood zone

The headline "33 million affected" is correct but impersonal. When you intersect the cumulative flood extent with WorldPop's 100 m population raster — division by division — the picture sharpens. The top 10 divisions held an estimated 25.9 million people inside the flooded zone. The single worst-hit was Larkana, Sindh: 5.78 million.

16 million children

Of the 33 million affected, 16 million were children — more than the entire population of Sweden or Cambodia. One in three flood casualties was a child — 615 children killed and 4,006 injured between June and September 2022.

8.2 million women were affected. 650,000 were pregnant. Of those, ~73,000 were full-term — expected to give birth within 30 days, often in flooded camps without clean water.

The flood didn't hit Pakistan evenly. It hit the parts already poorest — Dadu (57% household poverty), Dera Bugti (80% multi-dimensional poverty), Rajanpur (52%). The people who contributed least to the warming were the ones standing in the water.

7.9 million displaced — but mostly invisible

At peak, 7.9 million people were displaced — the largest internal displacement in Pakistan's history. But only 598,000 reached formal camps. The rest moved short distances — to higher ground, to relatives, to raised roads and railway embankments beside their flooded fields. Most of the displacement was invisible to satellites and to international media.

By end of 2023: 240,000 still displaced in Sindh alone. The five worst-hit districts (Dadu, Khairpur, Larkana, Nasirabad, Jaffarabad) accounted for 1,731,737 displaced by themselves per IOM's Displacement Tracking Matrix. As of May 2026: roughly 480,000 people remain in tents, mud-rebuild huts, or makeshift housing — counted as "returned" but not, by any meaningful definition, recovered.

The ecosystems — auto-tour

The map is now auto-touring through the four ecological zones that took the heaviest damage. Each one tells a different story:

  • Indus River dolphin habitat — Platanista minor, Endangered, ~1,800 left. The floods temporarily expanded their habitat then stranded six in irrigation canals when the water receded. WWF-Pakistan rescued them.
  • Sindh riverine forests — already down from 600,000 ha historically to under 100,000 ha. Adapted to flooding, but seedlings buried under sediment; survivors logged for fuel by displaced communities.
  • Manchar Lake (Asia's largest freshwater lake) — expanded from 250 km² to 520 km². The home of the Mohana fishing community, who live on houseboats; the breach drove them out and damaged the fishery.
  • Indus delta mangroves — for once, a small win. The freshwater pulse reached the dam-starved delta for the first time in decades; some mangrove regeneration was recorded post-flood.

Section 4 of 5

Who showed up — international and grassroots

The international system pledged $10.9 billion at Geneva in January 2023 and disbursed $2.8 billion by 2026. On the ground, Pakistani NGOs, local mutual-aid networks, fishers, religious communities, and women-led advocates moved faster than formal money. Toggle the layer panel to compare institutions, headquarters, and field response sites.

The international system

The UN issued a revised $816 million Flood Response Plan for Sep 2022 – May 2023. The largest single agency contribution was UNICEF's $173.5 million flash appeal for children. WHO put up $10 M for emergency health; IOM led shelter coordination reaching ~700k people; UNDP's Flood Recovery Programme has spent ~$65 M and built ~800 climate-resilient homes.

At the January 2023 Geneva conference, $10.9 billion was pledged — led by the Islamic Development Bank ($4.2 B), the World Bank ($2 B), ADB ($1.5 B), and bilateral support including Saudi Arabia ($1 B State Bank deposit), USAID ($200 M), and China (~$100 M direct + in-kind).

The grassroots — who rescued, fed, sheltered, rebuilt, and stayed

The international system was slow and concentrated in Karachi. The actual on-the-ground response came from a handful of Pakistani NGOs with verified accounting, plus thousands of mosque-, gurdwara-, and Hindu Panchayat-organised relief camps that never appeared in international donor reports.

The map now separates NGO headquarters from field response sites. Click the orange points to see what happened there, what still needs verification, and where a support pathway exists.

  • Edhi Foundation (Karachi) — ~1,800 ambulances, ~300 emergency relief camps across Sindh and Balochistan, food rations to ~400,000 families.
  • Saylani Welfare Trust (Karachi) — ~250,000 hot meals/day at peak from mobile kitchens in Dadu, Larkana, Sehwan.
  • JDC Foundation (Karachi) — first-wave boat rescues in Khairpur and Dadu.
  • Al-Khidmat Foundation (Lahore) — ~3,000 temporary shelters, ~50 medical camps, ~200 schools rebuilt by mid-2024.
  • Akhuwat Foundation (Lahore) — interest-free loans for flood-hit small businesses, ~PKR 1.2 billion deployed in first 6 months.
  • Pakistan Red Crescent (Islamabad) — ~150 health camps, ~50,000 family kits nationwide.

Pledged vs delivered — the asymmetry

By any honest measure, the grassroots NGOs delivered more direct beneficiary impact per dollar than the international system. They were also the ones who stayed — most international agencies wound down their Pakistan flood-specific programmes in 2023, while Edhi, Saylani, and Al-Khidmat continue rebuilding work today.

The asymmetry of who-pledges-vs-who-delivers became part of the broader climate-justice argument: the country put under water by other countries' emissions is also the one that had to organise its own response.

Sources: Humanitarian Outcomes critique paper · CDP 2022 Pakistan Floods overview.

Section 5 of 5

Recovery — and present day

PDNA need: $16.3 billion. Disbursed by May 2026: $2.8 billion. Homes destroyed: 2.1 million. Climate-resilient homes built under 4RF: about 800. The Loss & Damage Fund has disbursed nothing to Pakistani households.

The recovery that didn't happen

The World Bank's Post-Disaster Needs Assessment put reconstruction at $16.3 billion. At the January 2023 Geneva conference, donors pledged $10.9 billion. By May 2026 — three and a half years on — $2.8 billion has been disbursed.

Of the 2.1 million homes destroyed, only ~800 climate-resilient replacements exist under the 4RF framework. That is less than 0.04% of the need.

Why the gap

  1. Pledge-to-disbursement gap. Of $10.9 B pledged in Geneva, ~26% has actually been wired. Multilateral pledges are project-by-project loans that clear procurement and FX compliance in 12–18 months each.
  2. No national reconstruction budget. Pakistan's federal climate adaptation budget stayed under $8 M/year through FY24/25.
  3. The 2025 monsoon set the clock back. Floods in KP and Punjab damaged 38,000 additional houses and ~300,000 hectares of farmland.
  4. The flagship Sindh People's Housing programme stalled. 350,000 climate-resilient homes were to be delivered by end-2025. Actual delivery by May 2026: ~32,000.
  5. COP27 Loss & Damage Fund was established largely because of Pakistan. As of May 2026 it has disbursed nothing to Pakistani households.
Watch: Pakistan's lasting struggle — DW DocumentaryJan 2024

The 2025 monsoon, then likely a 2026 one — Pakistan is now in a permanent rolling-disaster posture. The question is no longer "can we recover from 2022" — it is "can we keep up with whatever comes next."

What this story is really about

This is not only a story about a natural disaster. It is a story about a country that contributed less than 1% of the warming, that was put under water by it, and that is still — three and a half years later — waiting for the rest of the world to keep its promises.

It is also the first EcoLens proof of concept for mapping response alongside risk: the ambulance drivers, the boat-volunteers, the Mohana fishermen, the Hindu Panchayat camps in Tharparkar, the Aurat Foundation organisers who made the camps safer for women, and the local groups still rebuilding after the cameras left.

The 800 homes that have been rebuilt are real homes. The 2,099,200 that haven't are also a number worth carrying.
